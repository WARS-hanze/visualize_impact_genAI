What does generative AI cost the planet?

Chatbots, image generators and coding assistants run on hardware in large data centres. Building that hardware and running it takes electricity, water and metals. Scroll to see what that added up to in 2024, one resource at a time.

Materials

Tonnes per year. Hardware mass becomes e-waste when retired. Copper goes into power distribution and cooling.

Scarcity-weighted view

Tonnes treat gold and steel alike. Life-cycle studies instead weight minerals by scarcity (abiotic depletion, kg Sb-eq). The only published LLM life-cycle study that does this is Mistral's. It found that server manufacturing causes 61% of Mistral Large 2's mineral depletion, versus 11% of its CO₂e and 5% of its water. Energy use accounts for 29%.

Source: Mistral/ADEME LCA 2025, from the infographic on Mistral's own page (the full report is not public). It covers one model on a low-carbon grid, so treat it as indicative. “Other” is mainly end-user devices (7% of minerals) and data-centre construction (1.5%); end-user devices are outside this page's scope. Manufacturing also dominates toxicity impacts (A100 LCA: 99% of cancer-toxicity, 85% of metal depletion).

Per request

Which kinds of AI use the most energy

No operator publishes how GenAI's total splits across chat, code, images or video. What exists are measured energy costs per request. Generating an image takes about 60 times as much energy as generating text, and around 1,000 times as much as classifying text.

kWh per 1,000 requests. Lab benchmarks: Luccioni et al. 2024 (88 models on A100 GPUs, measured 2023). Production figure: Google 2025 (median Gemini text prompt including idle capacity and cooling). The two methods differ, so compare within each group.

Method

How it is calculated, and what is left out

Sources are marked Checked when every figure used was matched against the original document, and carry † where a figure was taken second-hand. Where authors have a stake in the result (for example, a company reporting on its own products), it is stated.

Calculation chain

  • Data-centre electricity (IEA) × share on accelerated GPU/TPU servers (IEA) × generative-AI share of those servers gives GenAI's IT electricity.
  • × PUE adds cooling and facility overhead. × grid carbon intensity gives operational CO₂e.
  • IT electricity ÷ average draw per accelerator gives the fleet size. Fleet ÷ service life (or new units in a growing fleet) × embodied footprint per unit gives hardware impacts.
  • Facility electricity ÷ load factor gives built capacity. Capacity × embodied carbon and copper per MW, spread over 20 years, gives building impacts.
  • Water = on-site cooling (WUE) + power-plant water (per kWh) + chip-fab water per accelerator.

Left out

  • End-user devices and network transmission. Mistral found networks under 1%; devices are shared with everything else people do.
  • Generative AI running on phones and laptops, and on ordinary (non-accelerated) servers.
  • Data collection, labelling and staff.
  • Backup diesel generators, refrigerant leaks, and non-CO₂ effects.
  • Local water stress. A litre in a desert region matters more than a litre in a wet one.
  • Rebound effects and the emissions AI may help avoid elsewhere.

Confidence levels

HighReported or modelled by at least two independent sources that cover the whole quantity. MediumOne full source, or derived here by simple arithmetic from sourced numbers. LowExpert estimate for this page, with no direct measurement. The reasoning is shown.

Two ways to count hardware and buildings

Spread over life divides each server's and building's footprint by its lifetime, the usual convention in life-cycle studies. Built this year counts what is actually manufactured this year. In a fleet growing 30% a year, that is several times more.

Ranges

The low and high figures move every uncertain input to its low-impact or high-impact end at the same time. That makes them a deliberately wide bracket, not a statistical confidence interval. Inputs you change with a slider stay fixed at your value.
